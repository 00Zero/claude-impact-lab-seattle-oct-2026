# Principles for predicting the winning headline (within one article)

Ordered roughly by how often each one decided a test in the training set.

1. **Concrete scene over abstract issue.** The headline that shows a specific person doing a specific thing in a specific moment beats one that names the issue, the cause or the lesson.
   - e.g. "A Grandmother Opens Her Water Bill And Starts Laughing" beats "Why Utility Pricing Is A Matter Of Justice."
   - Does NOT hold when the concrete version is so narrow or obscure that the reader can't tell why it matters; a broad "these photos will change how you see X" can then win.

2. **Open a curiosity gap, but anchor it.** Give a vivid, concrete setup and withhold only the payoff (what was said, what happened, why); the gap must point at something specific.
   - e.g. "What A Shy Intern Told The CEO Left Him Speechless" beats "An Intern Teaches A CEO About Humility."
   - Does NOT hold for empty teasers with no setup ("You Won't Believe This"), or overt "you'll never guess" phrasing, which usually lose to a gap that is anchored in a real detail.

3. **Two beats: setup, then turn.** Headlines built as an expectation followed by a reversal, irony or escalation ("X. Then Y." / "X, but Y" / "So naturally…") beat single flat statements.
   - e.g. "Everyone Laughed At His Tiny Garden. Then The Drought Hit." beats "A Man Grows Food In A Small Garden."
   - Does NOT hold when the second beat merely restates the first or is a tired moral ("…and it's beautiful"); among near-identical two-beat variants, the sharper, more specific turn wins.

4. **Promise a satisfying payoff.** Signal that the reader will see a perfect comeback, a takedown, a backfire, or someone getting exactly what they deserved.
   - e.g. "A Heckler Interrupts Her Talk. Her Reply Ends The Argument." beats "A Speaker Discusses Hecklers."
   - Does NOT hold when the payoff is pre-announced as the writer's own rant ("Let me rant about…") or is aimed at insulting a named target; the payoff must be delivered by someone in the story.

5. **Name a real emotional reaction, ideally a specific one.** Headlines that state a felt reaction (devastated, made her cry, made me furious, totally made my day) beat neutral descriptions; a short first-person reaction can tip near-identical variants.
   - e.g. "I Didn't Expect A Spreadsheet To Make Me This Angry" beats "A Spreadsheet Shows Rising Rent Costs."
   - Does NOT hold for generic hype adjectives stacked without content (heartwarming, touching, adorable, amazing) or when the reaction replaces the story entirely ("This made me cry" with no subject).

6. **Statements beat abstract questions.** A declarative headline describing a situation beats a rhetorical or "what if / why / should we" question about an issue.
   - e.g. "This Town Banned Cars From Its Center And Shops Doubled Their Sales" beats "What If Cities Didn't Need Cars?"
   - Does NOT hold when the question invites the reader to guess something concrete ("Guess which job gets you hate mail just for…") or speaks to a familiar personal frustration ("Ever wonder why your landlord…?").

7. **Unexpected pairing or paradox.** Putting two things together that don't belong (a serious problem explained with a children's toy, a good thing that turns out harmful, a villain who is the hero) beats a straightforward description.
   - e.g. "A Box Of Crayons Explains The Housing Crisis Better Than Any Economist" beats "An Explanation Of The Housing Crisis."
   - Does NOT hold when the pairing is only a pun or wordplay rather than a real incongruity in the content (see 8).

8. **Plain, literal wording over puns, cute jokes and internet-speak.** Clear sentences beat clever wordplay, meme-speak, mock-baby-talk and strained metaphors.
   - e.g. "A Teacher Turned Her Classroom Into A Free Food Pantry" beats "Lunch Is Served: This Teacher's Class Act Is Souper."
   - Does NOT hold for mild innuendo or a cheeky double meaning that itself creates curiosity; those sometimes win.

9. **Show, don't preach or lecture.** Describing what happened beats telling the reader what to think, scolding them, addressing a target with sarcasm ("Dear X, have you tried…"), or editorializing about who is bad.
   - e.g. "A Mother Reads Her Son's Last Text Message To Lawmakers" beats "Lawmakers Need To Stop Ignoring Grieving Parents."
   - Does NOT hold when the opinion is framed as a definitive, shareable answer ("The Best Reason Why X Is Wrong", "The Perfect Response To…"), which acts like principle 4.

10. **Numbers: use them for list sizes and dramatic outcomes, not as dry statistics.** "7 things / 8 photos" and a single striking result ("cut deaths by 70%") help; percentages, dates and ratios presented as data lose.
    - e.g. "How One Small Change Cut A School's Dropout Rate In Half" beats "Dropout Rates Fell From 22% To 11% Between 2009 And 2013."
    - Does NOT hold when the number makes the story human-scaled and surprising (a huge population that vanished, a tiny number left); then the number can be the hook.

11. **Labels and shouting don't carry a headline.** Prefixes like "WATCH:", "NSFW:", "MUST SEE:", all-caps exclamations and "OMG" rarely help and often lose to the same idea stated plainly.
    - e.g. "A Firefighter Goes Back For The Family Cat" beats "MUST WATCH: Firefighter HERO Saves Cat!!!"
    - Does NOT hold for near-duplicate tests where the only difference is a stronger emotion word or superlative; there the more intense version often wins.

12. **Among near-duplicates, prefer the fuller, more specific, more human version.** When variants differ only slightly, the one with a clearer subject ("A young woman" over "She", "this guy" over "this clip"), a complete two-part structure, or a precise payoff word wins.
    - e.g. "A Retired Nurse Saw Her Neighbors Struggling And Did Something Nobody Expected" beats "She Saw Neighbors Struggling And Helped."
    - Does NOT hold when the extra words are filler or hedges; adding length without adding a concrete detail or stronger stake does not help.

## 3-step procedure for comparing headlines

1. **Screen for losers.** Mark down any headline that is mainly an abstract question, a dry statistic, a pun or meme-joke, a lecture/sarcastic address, or a label-plus-hype line (principles 6, 8, 9, 10, 11).
2. **Score the rest on the big four.** For each remaining headline, check: concrete person-in-a-scene (1), anchored curiosity gap (2), setup-then-turn (3), and a promised payoff or named emotion (4, 5); add a point for a genuine incongruity (7). Higher total wins; principles earlier in the list break ties.
3. **Resolve near-duplicates.** If the top candidates share the same core idea, pick the one that is more specific and human, has a cleaner two-beat structure, and uses the stronger emotional or payoff word (12, with 11's exception) — then sanity-check against the "does NOT hold" notes before deciding.
